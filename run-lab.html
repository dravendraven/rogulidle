Reference — how the game works

Combat

A blow always goes attacker → defender and there is no counter-attack: only whoever moved gets to strike. A duel is therefore strictly alternating, and the one who lands the first blow has a real edge.

The defender never enters the damage formula. Armour is a second bar rather than damage reduction, so how tough a target is changes how many blows they survive, never how hard a blow lands.

Monsters get no weapon bonus from what they carry — their loot lives in a drop field, not an inventory, so a dragon holding an axe still swings for its own xp.

Creature table

xp is both the damage stat and the number drawn above the head. activation is how close the hero must be before it wakes and gives chase. The last column is what actually predicts the cost of killing it.

Which roster is harder

Summing xp predicts nothing — six rats and one genie both total 6, and one costs nothing while the other costs twenty hp. A strong creature hits harder and lasts longer, and those multiply, so individual strength scales cost quadratically while count scales it linearly. That is why creature count is the dial.

Map generation

A 32×32 grid dug by ROT.js, then populated in a fixed order. The order matters: each step removes the tile it used from the free pool, so shuffling it changes every map.

Depth is the spine of generation. It is path distance, not straight-line distance, so a room two tiles away through a long corridor counts as far. Everything placed on the map is then scaled by it.

Creature placement

Strength is that strength term, and it is the one input whose name says least about it. It does not make creatures tougher; it decides how far up the table the deepest corner of the map can reach. At 0.35 the far end of a floor tops out around index 3 (boar), so even a twenty-creature floor is made of small things. At 1.0 the far corner reaches the t-rex. Strength is fixed across floors on purpose — floors differ by how many creatures they hold, not by what kind.

Chest loot

This inverts Rogule's original, which made distant chests less likely to hold anything (spec quirk §9.3). Either way the probability sweeps 10%→100%; we point it so that walking deeper pays.

The spine and its detours

Nothing is dug differently for this. The digger already makes maps with a route from hero to shrine and rooms hanging off it; a classification pass reads that structure back. A room is spine when the hero→shrine path crosses its rectangle, side otherwise. Because it only reads, it cannot fail to produce a floor and it consumes no randomness.

Risk and reward are drawn separately, and that is the whole design. Sharing one bonus made every detour offer the same ratio — and a gamble with a fixed favourable ratio is not a gamble, it is a free lunch that is always right to take. Measured that way, forbidding the detour, requiring it and permitting it produced identical dungeons.

The split is by mass, not headcount: cost tracks hp × (xp−1), so a floor can put 70% of its bodies on the route and still hide the dangerous half in a side room. Placement is greedy against a running share, so it converges without knowing the roster total in advance.

Loot and scarcity

The two sources hold different things, by design: chests give weapons and armour — gear comes from exploring — and corpses give health potions only — healing comes from killing. Both use the same scarcity rule.

Rogule filled the leftover space with chestnuts, mushrooms and gems: junk that existed only for the share card, and 53% of all loot. They are gone. The dilution they caused was doing real work, so it stayed — as an empty slot you set deliberately instead of a side effect of what was in the deck.

The difficulty model

Chests are flat by default, and that is the load-bearing choice. Tie them to the creature count and loot grows exactly as fast as threat — but the hero accumulates while each floor's threat is spent once, so the hero wins. Measured that way, floor ten handed over 64 items and capacity reached 118 against a starting 10. Set chests per creature above 0 below to watch it happen.

Reading the results

net challenge = damage the floor dealt ÷ capacity the hero walked in with. At 1.00 the floor costs exactly everything they had. This is measured, not modelled: it counts what actually happened, so being swarmed, cornered or hit while crossing a room is all in there. The earlier modelled version priced clean one-on-one duels and read 0.23 on a floor that killed four heroes out of seven.

capacity is the column to watch. If it climbs faster than damage does, the hero is outgrowing the dungeon and the descent gets easier no matter what the floors do. It should fall.

reached is the honest sample size. Deep floors are measured only on heroes who got that far, because those are the only heroes who ever meet them — a net challenge off two runs means much less than one off thirty. This survivor bias is not a flaw here; it is the player's real experience. For a floor measured in isolation, use floorHardness, which drops a fixed reference hero straight into it.
